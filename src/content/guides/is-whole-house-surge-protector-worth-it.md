## What a whole house surge protector actually does

A surge is a very short spike in voltage. Some come from outside: a lightning strike near the lines, or the utility switching equipment. Others start inside your home when a big motor, like an air conditioner compressor or a furnace blower, kicks on or off. Small surges add up and can wear down appliance electronics over time.

A whole house surge protector, also called a surge protective device or SPD, is mounted at or right next to your panel. When a spike comes in on the power wires, it sends the extra energy toward ground before it spreads through the branch circuits. That's why the grounding path at your panel matters as much as the device itself.

There are three common types:

- **Type 1:** installed on the line side, before the main breaker. Often used at the meter or service.
- **Type 2:** installed on the load side, in or next to the panel. This is what most homes get.
- **Type 3:** point-of-use protection, like a surge strip or a surge outlet.

Code calls for a Type 1 or Type 2 device at a home's service. Type 3 strips are still worth keeping as a second layer.

## When South Dakota already requires one

This part gets skipped in most national articles. South Dakota inspects electrical permits under the 2023 National Electrical Code, which has been in effect for permits since November 12, 2024, according to the South Dakota Electrical Commission. That code requires a surge protective device on the service for homes, and the requirement applies when service equipment is replaced, not only in new houses.

So if you're upgrading your panel, changing your service or building in Sioux Falls, Brandon, Harrisburg or Tea, the surge device is part of the job, not an upsell. If your panel isn't being changed, adding one is your call, and that's where the rest of this guide helps.

## Step by step: deciding if it's worth it for your house

None of these steps needs a tool or the panel cover off.

1. **Walk the house and list what's hard-wired.** Write down the furnace, air conditioner, water heater controls, range, dishwasher, washer, dryer, well or sump controls, garage door opener and any hot tub. These are the things a power strip can't cover.
2. **Look at your panel label and age.** Note the brand and whether there are open breaker spaces. Snap a photo of the label. Don't take the cover off. That photo tells an electrician if a device will fit.
3. **Look for an existing device.** Some homes already have one mounted next to the panel or built into a breaker. It usually has a small indicator light. If it's there and lit normally, you may only need it checked.
4. **Think about what's coming.** Planning a panel upgrade, a service change, an EV charger, a standby generator or a hot tub? The surge device is easiest to add during that work, and on a new or replaced service it's required.
5. **Remember the other ways surges get in.** Cable, internet and phone lines can carry a surge too. A panel device covers power wires only, so keep a good strip with coax or network protection on your modem and TV.
6. **Get the panel checked, not guessed at.** An electrician confirms the right device for your panel, that the ground path is there and how the device gets mounted, and gives you a written scope before work starts.
7. **Check the indicator after storms.** Once it's installed, glance at the light after a big storm and a few times a year. A failed light means it's time for a check, not that the house is unprotected forever.

## Checklist: signs a panel surge protector makes sense

If two or more of these fit your home, a whole house device is likely worth it:

- [ ] Furnace, AC or heat pump with a circuit board
- [ ] Smart or high-end appliances (range, fridge, washer, dryer)
- [ ] EV charger, hot tub, well pump or sump pump controls
- [ ] Home office, network gear or security system you rely on
- [ ] Panel being replaced, or a service change coming up
- [ ] More than one device died at the same time after a storm
- [ ] A panel surge device with a dark or fault indicator light

## Whole house surge protector vs power strip

| | Whole house surge protector | Plug-in surge strip |
|---|---|---|
| What it covers | Every circuit fed by the panel, including hard-wired equipment | Only what's plugged into it |
| Where it goes | At or next to the electrical panel | At the outlet |
| Who installs it | An electrician | You |
| Hard-wired furnace, AC, range | Yes | No |
| Cable, internet and phone lines | No (power wires only) | Some models, if you plug those lines in |
| Surges made inside the home | Helps for surges that pass the panel | Helps at that one outlet |
| Required by code | On new or replaced home services | No |
| When to replace | When the indicator shows a fault, or after a big hit | When its light goes out or it's years old |

They work best together: the panel device takes the brunt on the power lines, and the strip handles what's left at your TV or computer.

## What a whole house surge protector can't do

Knowing the limits makes the decision easier:

- **It can't promise to stop a direct lightning strike.** No device can.
- **It doesn't fix a bad ground or a damaged panel.** If the ground path is missing, it has to be fixed first or the device can't do its job.
- **It doesn't protect cable, internet or phone lines.** Those need their own protection.
- **It doesn't last forever.** The parts inside wear down as they absorb surges. A big hit can use one up in a single storm, which is why the indicator light matters.

## FAQs

### Is a whole house surge protector worth it if I already use power strips?

Usually, yes. Strips only guard what is plugged into them. The furnace, air conditioner, range, washer, dryer and garage door opener are wired or plugged in where a strip can't help. Keep your strips on TVs and computers and add the panel device for the rest.

### Will a whole house surge protector stop lightning damage?

No device can promise that. A direct strike can still damage equipment. A panel surge protector is built for surges that come in on the power lines, such as a nearby strike or a utility switching event, plus the smaller surges made inside your home.

### Is a whole house surge protector required in South Dakota?

For new or replaced services, yes. South Dakota inspects permits under the 2023 National Electrical Code, which requires a surge protective device on services supplying homes. A house with an older panel that isn't being changed generally isn't forced to add one.

### How do I know if my surge protector still works?

Most panel devices have an indicator light. Check it after big storms and a few times a year. If the light shows a fault or is out, the device may have used itself up and should be checked and replaced. Don't open the panel yourself.

### Can a surge protector be added to my existing panel?

Often, yes. It usually needs an open breaker space or a side mount, plus a working ground path. A full, damaged or outdated panel may need repair or an upgrade first. An electrician checks this before anything is installed.
